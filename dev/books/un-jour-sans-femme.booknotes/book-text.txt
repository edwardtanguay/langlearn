## Prologue

It is there in the fingers untying the apron, removing the smock—that uniform of the over-tight file—there in the hand resting the head near the control panel; the need to wash away the bistro grime, the urge to let go; it is in the hands closing the supermarket till, lowering the shutter at the bank, the post office, the metro, the gas station, the toll booth; it is there in the hands holding the headset and microphone for the broadcast, the announcements, the safety instructions on the plane, or unplugging the switchboard; it is that moment of transition—actress leaving the stage, singer shedding the costume—cashing out as one sheds a heavy burden; a gladiator abandoning the queen, a soldier leaving the ranks; that is where the world empties out; that is where the world stands on its own two feet.

## Chapitre 1

There will never again be carefreeness, never again joy; the child will never again be young after this. The moment she learns of Sofia's death, her world shatters.

It was a fresh start for her. She was 26—wait, no, she was much younger—when her parents divorced and they had to move. She joined the school mid-year, by which time social groups had already formed and friendships were established; any hope of making friends seemed slim. She felt like a 3kg bag of those mega-sized liquorice candies—just a small, odd thing that didn't quite fit in.

Sofia is the first to approach—the hungrier, shyer one; she accepts a treat, right as a tooth—specifically an incisor—comes loose. Caught between shock and excitement, she prepares to show Léane: "Look, the Tooth Fairy is coming tonight and will leave you some money." Her mother, meanwhile, looks on with a touch of dismay.

Fish, bad luck, your body, all those baby teeth—and you’re like a piggy bank of hopes, truly empty yet naturally emphatic. Sofia generously offers to safeguard the loot; she has a better idea—a scheme to double the haul. Once the coins are stashed away, Sofia will retrieve the precious prize and lend it out. Krakla’s mother—often absent and always in a rush—won’t take the time to inspect the mouth; simply showing the can to trigger a smile will do the trick. It’s a sure thing—a win-win situation with impeccable logic: you get twice the return if you put in thirty-two times the money.

Sophia, your reasoning is absolutely brilliant; I’m in awe of your boldness—one day you’ll be president. You really know how to work the system, even with the older kids in class—like that whole racket involving cable discs. It’s amazing how you manage to pull it all off. And when your parents refuse to give you money, you find a way—though the guys immediately confiscate whatever is left, since most of the earnings went into buying candy. That little girl—who’s even more wary than you—was born into this; I certainly never had a dull moment with my classmates. So, out of pity, Sophia offers to stop by the shop.

From that day on, their destinies were intertwined; Kapla and Sofia grew together like ivy on a wall, inextricably linked. They chose the same electives, gave the same class presentations, and signed up for the same activities. They even wore the same braces and caught COVID at the same time. At sixteen, they fell for the same boy—dating him in turn before eventually losing interest. Far from driving them apart, the experience brought them even closer, turning them into "sister-wives"—a local term for two women who love the same man.

Coming from a family where her mother was often away abroad, she found—in Sofia—far more than just a friend; she found a lifelong companion and a home. She would often spend weeks at a time at Sofia’s place, and in the summer, they would stay together at Sofia’s grandparents' farm. She spent her time running among the sheep, playing in the fields, and knitting little hats.

Both only children, they decided—at the age of 14, during an impromptu ceremony in a sheepfold—to officially become sisters, vowing to protect each other for life; they tied matching bracelets around their wrists—real friendship bracelets—promising to replace them whenever they happened to break.

Of course, such a friendship is not without its rough patches; there are a few cracks, attempts by unwanted third parties to intrude, and minor betrayals that ultimately prove inconsequential. The relationship withstands the onslaught, however, thanks to mutual support and a balance of forces—Sofia’s gentleness tempering your father’s volcanic energy. One is moderate and cautious, the other impulsive and extroverted; yet, despite these differences, those around them agree that they eventually came to resemble one another—even sharing the same taste in buildings and hairstyles.

When they started university, they moved onto the campus together, each pursuing a different field of study—one in sociology, the other in educational sciences. The first aimed for a career in research, while the second aspired to teach. Their life goals differed as well: Sophie wanted to start a family, whereas the other had no interest in a relationship—she refused to compromise her freedom for a partner. While she didn't mind solitude, she dreaded the kind of emotional dependency that leads to alienation and submission. This sparked endless debates, with Sophie trying to convince her friend of the merits of a stable relationship; yet her friend remained unmoved, insisting she needed no one but Sophie.

The only fly in the ointment—or rather, the shadow on the scene—was a friction that slipped in like an impurity; things got too tight. They had met at the Iceland Airwaves music festival while working as technicians. Amidst the city's buzz and a Pink Logo concert, he offered to take them backstage to meet the musicians—an easy, perhaps questionable, pick-up tactic. Yet, he approached her. Since that day, Sophie has been with him, despite the hopes others harbored for the young man—though some found him an unlikable character. He was charming and popular, capable of spending hours talking about the Icelandic music scene, which he considered one of the most dynamic in the world. Their lives became inextricably intertwined; whenever they met, it was like two animals vying for Sophie’s attention. She eventually took a side, preferring to see them apart. Gone were the days when the two friends shared a mutual love for the same girls; the man who could successfully drive a wedge between them had yet to be born.

On this morning, Craquelin got up early to go for a run—training for the 2008 Midnight Sun race, held at the solstice when the sun barely sets, lingering in the sky with an indefinable hue. These endless days bring a magical energy; she has more drive and appetite, rising two hours earlier than usual and going to bed later—a welcome shift during exam season. She knows every moment counts; in a few months, the sun will yield to the darkness. Her mind, a mix of bipolar and cyclothymic moods, is wired in a surprisingly unique way.

As usual, he leaves the apartment while Sophie is still asleep; she has to go into his room to pull him out of bed as the alarm clock goes off.

As she runs, she mentally reviews the key points of the thesis she must defend—a project covering topics ranging from 2017 equal pay legislation to Icelandic healthcare design. This presentation will secure her Master's degree, a milestone she hopes will launch her career. She has worked tirelessly, pouring her heart and soul into it, often toiling through the quiet hours of the night—a solitary, intense process.

A measure from March 8, 2017, requires Icelandic companies with 25 or more employees to prove perfect wage equality between men and women. The text is innovative: the burden is no longer on women to discover they are earning less than their male colleagues; instead, companies must demonstrate that any pay gaps are not linked to gender. Companies failing to do so face sanctions—a first on an international scale.

Iceland boasts the world's highest employment rate for women and is considered a model of equality; according to surveys, it is also the happiest place to live. Studies have shown that a more equal society leads to higher life expectancy and a better standard of living. To conclude this presentation.

At the university, things played out just as they did for her colleague; because she answered the professors' questions with confidence—and even a touch of humor that the jury seemed to appreciate—she was congratulated, her defense was approved, and she secured her doctorate for the start of the academic year.

That evening, he meets Sophie at Le Louvre—their favorite club—to celebrate the success. Upon hearing the news, however, he is visibly agitated; he had expected Sophia to accompany him to the Bresse-Landes festival, where he is due to work in July. Sophie admits that while things started happily enough at the beginning of their relationship, she has recently felt a growing tension—a sense of unease that betrays her true feelings, tinged with an anxiety that nothing seems to soothe.

The discussion is interrupted by a group entering the club; one can spot the insignia—everyone here recognizes the first-aid association—while the proceedings are broadcast on television. Such events—natural disasters—are common in the region.

Forget Bienne, we'll find you a sensor before 4 a.m., launches Rapta, leading her on foot towards rescuers. She likes this expression which refers to a one-night stand, met before the nightclub closes. Shy Sofia protests. The four of them, full of Neil, don't give her a choice. She moves towards the table and the group, essentially male and silly, is settled in. She rests to protect you in glass. First aid doesn't need to be asked twice. Together, the train to life, to love, to friendship, to attract the attention of girls and men. Yoga, it's a suitcase, a joke.
