At an age when others were out having fun, she devoted the best years of her youth to her studies, making sacrifices from the heart; she was driven by a deep-seated conviction that it was all worth it.

While she is proud of the path she has traveled, her daily life is far removed from what she had imagined; it is an uphill battle that renews itself every day. Of course, there are moments of grace—smiles and looks that speak volumes—and the unforgettable, profound feeling of having been useful and exactly where she belongs.

There are magical moments when everyone's information comes together—moments of calm focus that save a life—and a spirit of brotherhood within the team, united and standing together in the face of adversity.

Over time, certain colleagues became like family to her. One nurse, in particular, became her best friend; when she first joined the department, this colleague helped her find her bearings, warning her about potential pitfalls and advising her on which doctors to keep at arm's length. As for the department head—who used to summon her early just to ask her to make coffee—the solution was simply to pour a little salt into his cup; a strategy that quickly paid off.

Regarding her background—specifically her qualifications and salary—some took advantage of the situation to assign her demanding tasks that others sought to avoid; meanwhile, everyone here has spoken about the nurse and her feminist convictions.

At night, she would go out to put up posters in the streets of Paris to denounce films that promote femicide; she wasn't afraid to go out on these nocturnal rounds. But today she is too tired to go out. Just yesterday, at PL's wedding, she was chosen as a witness; she is also the godmother of their first child—a little boy, no more than four years old.

Her shift was drawing to a close, and she was writing up her final notes. She had to calculate the dosage—three units of a twelve-unit product—but was worried about making a mistake; a few months earlier, a colleague had made an error at the end of a 24-hour shift by writing "insulin" instead of "morphine" on a prescription. Fortunately, the nurse responsible for administering the injection had a moment of doubt and managed to catch the colleague before she left the hospital to clarify the order.

While performing the procedure, the senior nurse suffered a sudden health crisis; without her colleague, the patient would likely have died.

After the handover to the morning shift, she keeps her scrubs on and promises herself she’ll stop by to see Augustine—her third little one since the start of the school year—during the day. Then she heads to her car in the parking lot; she usually takes public transport, but with four-hour delays announced, she planned ahead.

As she passes, she inhales a deep breath of fresh air—which seems delicious to her, despite the pollution—leaving behind the hospital’s atmosphere, a mix of body odors and the sharp tang of detergents. Before setting off, she turns her phone back on and sees a message from the lawyer she’s been seeing lately; she met him in the ER one night when he came in with a broken arm after slipping on a patch of ice. They had struck up a conversation while she was setting the cast—a pleasant moment, a welcome respite in the middle of an endless day. He had left his number on a scrap of paper.

After hesitating for a long time about reconnecting—spurred on by the hope that she was single—he finally decided to invite her for a drink; they talked and flirted. For their second date, they went to see an exhibition at the Maison des Mondes Africains. Later, he suggested they have dinner at his place, boasting about his cooking skills, though they were both overcome by a sudden, nervous panic.

So, she actually likes him—he’s cultured, attentive, and quite charming; she can’t fault him for anything. Yet, she dreads yet another relationship—or the "morning after" scenario where a man (even a white one) wakes up realizing his time with a Black woman was just an experiment. Sometimes she feels like a curiosity, a canvas onto which he projects his fantasies and clichés. She’s tired of the endless hours spent on dating sites, the constant vetting, and the exhausting search for the right match.

The men she meets are usually disappointing, and things rarely go any further; she employs various defensive strategies because she is afraid of commitment. It is true that the situation is not easily deciphered: in an emergency setting, you never see the patients again—which suits her perfectly, as they either go home or are admitted to specialized wards after treatment, so no lasting relationships develop. It seems the same pattern holds true in her personal life; at nearly thirty-five, she has built nothing outside of her career and her small circle of friends.

She considers how to decline the invitation; she leaves Bobigny and takes the 86 towards Montreuil, where she lives. Images of the night's patients flash through her mind—people she saw suffering, screaming, and dying. She is weighed down by an exhaustion that makes her skull feel like it’s splitting and makes her want to vomit. Her eyes begin to close, just slightly—a mere second, a fleeting moment—and she falls asleep at the wheel.

She rises; one feels a sudden, terrifying jolt—a moment where heaven and earth seem to flip upside down. Her car has crossed the hard shoulder and smashed through the crash barrier; inside the cabin, every second feels like an eternity.

Physical reality takes hold as she stabilizes in the ditch; the repetition of the moment makes her realize she is still alive. As the fire siren wails, she grasps that her life has just been turned upside down—as if she were a character suddenly struck by a flash of lightning and erased from the other side.

Regarding the aftermath of the accident: the person cannot get up—unable to leave the bed or return to work. It’s a miracle—the firefighters said so—considering the state of the crushed sheet metal; the vehicle was mangled, yet the person, though in shock, survived. The medical assessment—specifically the S1 classification—focuses essentially on the psychological impact rather than physical injuries.

The accident—predictable, almost inevitable, she tells herself—a car veering off the road, foreshadowed by a headache she had long ignored and failed to name amidst the daily suffering she faces; in the urgency of the moment, she had downplayed it—perhaps forgiving it like background noise or a commercial jingle. For months, she has been rising with a knot in her stomach, trying to keep it all hidden.

The mere thought of walking through the hospital doors fills her with a touch of anxiety; one night, she woke up feeling as though her chest were in a vice—unable to breathe. Sometimes she dreams she is walking along the edge of a precipice, watching herself fall.

She can't help but think of that night-shift nurse—someone who had worked at the ER reception desk for years. One morning, she was found in an exam room next to a syringe of insulin she had just injected; her suicide shook the hospital. The tragedy even became the subject of a news report on the mental health crisis among healthcare workers—a reality often linked to the doctors themselves—yet nothing has changed since.

Lying in bed, she no longer wants to lie to herself; she has exhausted her reserves of energy, patience, and vitality. She is like her car, abandoned on the roadside—shattered and at a standstill. Her apartment feels empty and alien around her; life itself seems to have withdrawn. She knows the only place where she can rebuild and rediscover herself is the village where she was born.

## Chapitre 4 : Bario San José, Soyapango, El Salvador

110 pairs of sleeves an hour, ten hours a day—she had no formal education, yet she worked away; it adds up to a lifetime spent in the shed housing the garment workshop, illuminated by the void, stitching fabric in rhythm from morning till night—uniforms destined for major American universities, for prestigious institutions thousands of miles away, places she has never set foot in.
